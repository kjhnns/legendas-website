Legenda: live transcription that runs on your iPhone

Your iPhone writes down what is said around it, while it is said. A translation appears below when you want one. Speech recognition and translation run on the phone.

Johannes Klumpe · Technical note · November 2026

Abstract. Legenda is live transcription on the iPhone. The phone writes down what is said around it and shows the words as captions, with an optional translation below. You can read earlier captions, then return to the live line. The transcript becomes your notes. After setup, speech recognition and translation run on the phone, without a network. The app does not upload the conversation. Text leaves the app only when you share it, copy it or export a diagnostic log. Six languages can share one conversation. This note sets out the evidence and the limits.

2 Claim 1Speech becomes text while it is spoken

The phone lies on the table and listens through its microphone. Captions show what is said. Turn on Translate to split the screen: captions above, translation below in yellow. One menu selects the target language.

Translation works on finalized caption lines. A line ends at a pause or at a length limit, so it can end in the middle of a sentence. The translation therefore trails the caption. It can also wait for a language download or for earlier lines. A line already in the target language appears unchanged. The screen stays awake while the app listens.

In the author's field logs from the phone used for development, a recognition pass took about 0.2 to 0.3 seconds.1 Captions updated about every 1.5 seconds. A pass time measures recognition work, not the delay from speaking a word to seeing it.

3 Claim 2Earlier lines stay within reach

Scroll up to read again what was said a minute ago. New lines do not pull you back while you read older captions. Tap Live, or scroll to the bottom, to return to the live line. Each pane scrolls on its own.

4 Claim 3The transcript is the notes

You do not have to write the words down again. Share transcript opens the iOS share sheet for Notes, Files, Mail or another app that accepts text. Copy transcript puts the same text on the clipboard.

The transcript contains the captions, not the translation. It is plain text, one line per caption line. It retains up to the most recent 20,000 finalized lines, plus the current unfinished line when you export. If older lines have been dropped, the export says so.

5 Claim 4Everything runs on the phone after setup

Speech recognition uses NVIDIA Parakeet, the FluidInference/parakeet-ultra-coreml build, through the open-source FluidAudio library. It runs on the iPhone's Apple Neural Engine. Silero VAD detects voice activity. Apple's Translation framework translates on the device.

First start needs a network connection. Setup downloads about 630 MB for the speech model from Hugging Face, plus about 2 MB for voice detection. It then prepares the speech model for the Neural Engine. iOS downloads a translation language pair the first time that pair is needed. With these assets installed, captions and translation work in airplane mode.

6 Claim 5You decide what leaves the phone

There is no account, no sign-in and no server of ours. The app has no analytics or crash-reporting SDK. Its own network code fetches model files and setup metadata. It does not upload the conversation.

Audio is processed in memory and is not saved. The transcript also lives in memory. It is lost when the app process closes unless you have shared or copied it elsewhere. Switching away from the app is not the same as closing its process.

One exception is explicit. A hidden diagnostics panel has the action Export log (includes captions). It writes a temporary text file with diagnostic events and caption text, and opens the share sheet for it. This happens only when you tap it. The app never saves a session by itself.

Pause and continue use one button. Sharing the transcript, copying it, and exporting a diagnostic log are your actions. Tell the people at the table that your phone is captioning the conversation.

Table 4. Supported languages and test coverage. The speech test measures recognition, not translation quality.

The speech model was tested on FLEURS read speech: 100 utterances each in Brazilian Portuguese, German and US English. The noisy condition adds babble at 10 dB signal-to-noise ratio and reverberation to the same utterances. Word error rate counts substitutions, deletions and insertions relative to the reference words. Lower is better.

A separate, small test used synthetic speech with mixed languages. In 29 of 37 checks the expected English word was present in the recognized text. The test checks that the word is present, not where it stands. It is indicative only. Names and loanwords can be wrong.

8 LimitsLegenda is a reading aid

  • Hardware. iPhone only, with iOS 26 or later. Tested on one chip so far.1
  • Setup. First start needs a network connection and several minutes. New translation pairs need a download. An update can trigger model preparation again.
  • Accuracy. There are six supported languages. In the Mac test, noise raised word error rate from 3.55% to 7.64% for Portuguese, 3.74% to 11.35% for German, and 5.11% to 6.71% for English. Names and loanwords can be wrong.
  • Speakers. Legenda does not say who is speaking.
  • Translation. It works on finalized caption lines, not word by word, so it trails the captions.
  • Heat and battery. The Neural Engine works throughout listening. The phone gets warm and uses battery. The author's field logs showed the thermal state “serious” in long sessions; the app then decodes less often. Running memory use was about 1.3 GB.1
  • Use. Captions are a reading aid, not a certified record. Tell the people at the table that the phone is captioning them.
  • Maintenance. One person builds it.
